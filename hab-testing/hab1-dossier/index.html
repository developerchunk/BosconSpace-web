edge of space

A hydrogen balloon carries a Raspberry Pi to roughly 30 km above the Deccan plateau. From the ground we send it a prompt over LoRa. It runs a language model locally, signs the answer with its own key and its own altitude, and sends it back. Then it comes down under a parachute, and we go and find it.

01

The mission

One paragraph you should be able to recite, and three success criteria you are not allowed to move later.

Mission statement

Launch a hydrogen-filled latex sounding balloon carrying a self-contained compute payload to ≥ 25 km. While airborne, receive a text prompt transmitted from a ground station, run inference locally on an on-board small language model, and transmit the response back. Continuously downlink position and health telemetry. After burst, descend under parachute, keep reporting position, and physically recover the payload.

The reframe that governs every other decision

This is not a project to run an LLM at 25 km. Running a quantised 1.5B model on a Pi 5 at ~11 tokens/second is a solved problem you could demonstrate this afternoon. This is a tracking-and-recovery mission that happens to carry a computer. If the box does not come back you have no flight data, no video, no hardware and no second flight.

So the design order is recovery first, communications second, compute last — and that ordering is visible in every section below.

Success criteria

LevelCriterion
Minimum Reaches ≥ 15 km. Position telemetry received for ≥ 80% of flight. Payload physically recovered. On-board log shows at least one inference executed above 15 km.
Full Minimum, plus ≥ 25 km, plus at least one complete prompt→response round trip completed over the radio link above 20 km, with the response cryptographically signed by the payload.
Extended Full, plus ≥ 5 round trips, plus ≥ 1 downlinked image from ≥ 20 km, plus drone-assisted terminal localisation demonstrated.

Note that recovery sits inside the minimum criterion. That is deliberate and it is the whole thesis of this document.

03

Clearance & launch window

Talks are open with Pune ATC. Two things about Pune change this mission more than any hardware decision: who you are talking to, and which way the wind blows in which month.

Who you are actually talking to

Pune Airport (PNQ / VAPO) is a civil enclave inside Lohegaon Air Force Station. The airfield is owned by the Indian Air Force, the AAI runs the civil terminal, and air traffic control is provided by the IAF. Your clearance is a military coordination, not a civil formality.

That is mostly good news. The people assessing your request work operational fast-jet movements for a living. They will not be moved by enthusiasm; they will be moved by a precise, quantified, conservative flight plan. And they care most about the 0–12 km band where their traffic is — not about 25 km. Frame the request around that transit and its timing.

Recommendation

Target mid-October to mid-November. Post-monsoon skies are clear, surface winds are light, fields are accessible, and upper-level winds are at their annual minimum — which directly shrinks both your landing ellipse and your chase distance. Secondary window: February–March.

Pune is unusually asymmetric — exploit it

Hard no-launch rule — write it down now

The Arabian Sea is ~130 km west. Your 90% drift corridor is 66–161 km. Those two numbers overlap.

If the predicted track has any westward component, do not fly. There is no recovery from the sea and none from the Ghats. This must not be a judgement call made on launch morning with a full hydrogen cylinder and a disappointed team standing around.

04

Architecture

One rule generates the entire design.

The governing principle

The thing that tells you where the payload is must not share a battery, a processor, an antenna, or a software stack with the thing that runs the LLM.

Your Raspberry Pi is the least reliable component in the system. It is the hottest, the hungriest, the most software-complex, and the most likely to hang, brown out or corrupt its filesystem. Do not let its failure cost you the payload. So: three chains, physically and electrically independent, in two boxes on the line.

Three decisions inside that diagram

The beacon always wins

The response is chunked precisely so it can be pre-empted by the position beacon. Never let a thirty-second response transmission block your only source of position data. Priority order is fixed in radiod: beacon > command ack > LLM response chunks > image chunks.

The uplink is authenticated

Anyone with a €20 LoRa module and your frequency can transmit to your payload. Without an HMAC, a passer-by — or a bored person on a forum who saw your launch announcement — can inject prompts, burn your duty-cycle budget, or trigger a command you did not intend. A monotonic counter blocks replay of a captured frame.

The response is signed on the payload

The Ed25519 private key exists only in the payload's flash. A signature over (response, lat, lon, alt, utc) is a cryptographic proof that this text was generated by that box, at that altitude. For a demonstration whose entire point is "an LLM ran at 25 km", that turns a claim into evidence. Publish the public key before launch, and anyone can verify it afterwards.

The design consequence

You have surplus margin at the fastest setting. Spend it on data rate, not on range. Because the binding constraint is not distance — it is India's 1% duty cycle in the 865–867 MHz band: 36 seconds of transmit time per hour, per channel. A 50-byte beacon at SF7/250k costs 36 ms; the same beacon at SF12/125k costs 1,810 ms. Same information, fifty times the budget.

Where the link actually fails

Plot expected signal against altitude and you get a U-shape: strong at apogee, weak at both ends. The end that matters is the second one.

Corollary

Your last received packet before losing the link is the most valuable data in the mission. The ground software must log every packet with a timestamp and continuously extrapolate a landing estimate from the last known position, altitude, descent rate and wind — so that the instant the link drops, you already have a coordinate to drive to.

And the sentence that matters, from the same manual

"If a sanity check against the limit of the dynamic platform model fails, the position solution becomes invalid."

Not degraded. Invalid. A receiver left in Portable stops producing a valid position at 12 km — lower even than the 18 km COCOM figure usually quoted, and less than half your burst altitude. You would lose position across apogee and most of the descent: the entire interesting part of the flight, and the part you need for recovery.

Set CFG-NAVSPG-DYNMODEL = 6 on both GPS units, save to flash, power-cycle, and read it back — it must survive the power cycle. Re-assert it at boot and every 60 s in flight. Verify the whole path in HIL with a synthetic 30 km stream. This is test L0.1 and it is the highest-priority test in the campaign.

A balloon ascends at 5 m/s and descends at up to 38 m/s — nowhere near the <1g model's limits. Take the tighter filter and the better accuracy.

08

Parts & wiring

Quantities, connectors, and the answer to the question everyone asks about LoRa modules first.

Do we need a receiver and a transmitter?

No — a LoRa module is a transceiver. One part does both. There is no separate receiver to buy, and no external transistor or amplifier to add: the power amplifier, the low-noise amplifier and the RF switch are all already on the module.

But a radio link needs a radio at each end. One in the payload, one on the ground. They are the same kind of part, taking turns — LoRa is half-duplex, so a module cannot transmit and receive in the same instant. That is why no quantity in the parts list below is ever 1.

Standardising on the E22-900M30S

Decision taken: one part number everywhere — the E22-900M30S in the payload, on the ground station, on the drone, and in the spares box. That is a defensible call, and it buys real things:

  • One footprint, one adapter board, one driver configuration, one spares pool. Fewer ways to grab the wrong part at 2 a.m. before a launch.
  • Commandable reserve power. This is the genuinely good argument. Fly at 22 dBm nominally, and if the link degrades on descent — the phase where it actually fails — you can uplink a command to raise power for the last few kilometres. You cannot do that with a module whose ceiling is 22 dBm.
  • Simpler procurement: one line item, one supplier, ₹929 each.

Transmit power is a software setting, not a property of the part. The 30S will happily run at 22 dBm or lower. So the question is not "which module" but "what do I have to design for", and the answer is the current draw.

Three things you must now do, because you are flying the 30S

The 650 mA figure is the reason. That pulse, on a battery-powered payload, is exactly what dips the rail, resets the Pi and corrupts a filesystem mid-write — failure F4 in the risk register. These three items move it from a risk to a non-issue, and none of them is optional now.

  1. Give the radio its own 5 V rail with a 1000 µF low-ESR capacitor mounted at the module. Not shared with the Pi's 5 V, not tapped off a GPIO pin. The capacitor supplies the pulse; the buck only has to supply the average.
  2. Set TX power to 22 dBm in flight software, as a default that must be explicitly commanded higher. You have +12 dB of margin at 250 km already. Keep the extra 8 dB as a reserve you deploy deliberately, not a level you sit at.
  3. Measure the actual current at your chosen power setting in test L0.2 — with a USB power meter and a scope if you can borrow one. The 650 mA is the datasheet's figure at 30 dBm; at 22 dBm it will be substantially lower, but the external PA still draws bias current, so it will not fall all the way to the 22S's 118 mA. Design the power budget on your measurement, not on either number.
And the legal ceiling has not moved

India caps ERP at 4 W (36 dBm). Owning a 1 W transmitter does not raise that. With a 10 dBi Yagi (≈ 7.85 dBd) your ground station must stay at or below ~28 dBm; with a 15 dBi Yagi, ~23 dBm. On the payload's ~2 dBi antenna you could legally run the full 30 dBm — which is precisely the reserve worth having for the descent phase. Put the calculation in your radiod config as a hard cap per antenna, so no one can accidentally command past it.

Do not power an E22-900M30S from an ESP32's 3.3 V pin

Dev boards use a small AMS1117-class regulator fed from USB. A 650 mA transmit pulse will collapse the rail and reset the ESP32 mid-packet, and you will spend a day blaming your code. Three rules the diagrams encode

Nothing crosses between chains. Not a battery, not a ground wire, not a switch. If you find yourself running one wire from Box A to Box B, stop and work out what you are actually trying to do.

Antennas point the right way. The 868 MHz payload antenna points down: a ground-plane vertical has its null along the axis, so pointing it down puts the null at the balloon and the gain at the horizon and the ground. The GNSS patch points up, at the satellites.

The wire never takes load. Every connector gets hot-glue strain relief and a cable tie anchoring the loom to the structure. Pull-test each one before it flies, and again after the vibration test.

How to verify antennas without a VNA

The VNA's real job here is catching one failure: an antenna that is not resonant at 868 MHz. Three ways to catch it without one:

  • Comparative RSSI, free. Put two radios a fixed distance apart and log RSSI while swapping antennas. A wrong-band or damaged antenna shows up as 10–20 dB down. This catches the failure mode that actually matters, and it uses hardware you are buying anyway.
  • RTL-SDR waterfall, free. You can see your own transmissions and confirm the centre frequency and that the module is keying at all.
  • RTL-SDR + noise source + directional coupler, ~₹2,000 if you later want real return-loss numbers. A tenth of the VNA price, same answer for this band.

And buy the payload antenna commercially from a manufacturer that tunes for 868 — that is the one you cannot swap once it is in the air. DIY the ground antennas, where you can test and iterate freely.

Why not one big battery for everything?

Because of one specific failure that this design exists to survive, and it is worth walking through rather than asserting.

The cabin sits near 0 °C at apogee because the Pi is dissipating ~6 W into it. The Pi's waste heat is the heating system. So consider what happens if the Pi dies at 25 km:

  • The heat source stops. The box begins cooling toward −55 °C ambient.
  • A shared Li-ion pack cools with it. Li-ion retains roughly half its capacity at −20 °C and is essentially dead at −40 °C.
  • So the tracker dies too: an hour or two after the event it exists to survive, and while the payload is still descending under parachute.

That is a common-mode failure: one root cause taking out both the mission and the thing meant to be independent of it. The L91s break it, because lithium-iron-disulfide is rated to −40 °C and does not care that the heater stopped. Four AA cells and about ₹1,000 to remove the single most plausible way of losing the payload permanently.

Two "simpler" options I rejected, and why

A USB power bank. Genuinely tempting: one sealed unit, 5 V out, no BMS to build. But power banks auto-shut-down when current draw drops below a threshold, which is exactly what your payload does between inferences; most cannot sustain 5 A; and their pouch cells behave worse in cold than cylindrical 21700s. A silent shutdown at 20 km is not a risk worth the convenience.

One large LiPo. A 3S 5000 mAh pouch is 55 Wh for ~380 g, but pouch cells are puncture-prone, worse in cold, and land badly. The 21700 cylindricals are tougher, and 2S1P of P45B already gives 1.7× margin at 140 g.

So the honest count is two packs and one sealed device: down from three converters and three packs, now that Chain 2's 6 V→5 V buck is gone. If you want it simpler still, the one further step available is dropping Chain 3, and that is the one I would argue hardest against: it is ₹3,300 and it is the thing most likely to simply end the search with a text message.

Why two chemistries

Li-ion has the energy density you need for a 6 W load, but its usable capacity collapses below 0 °C. L91 lithium-iron-disulfide has lower peak current capability but works at −40 °C.

So: energy-dense cells where it is warm, cold-tolerant cells where it is cold. Put the right chemistry in the right thermal zone rather than trying to make one chemistry do both jobs. Chain 2 must survive overnight if recovery slips to the next morning — and it will be sitting in a field at whatever temperature the field is.

Non-negotiable: every chain gets an independent battery, an independent fuse, and an independent power switch reachable from outside the sealed box — a magnetic reed switch or a pull-tab, so you can arm without opening the payload on the launch field.

About the thick white thermocol box

Thermocol is EPS — the right material — and white is the right colour. Two of the three decisions are correct. The trap is in the word thick.

Insulation protects you from the environment's heat. It does nothing about your own, and in fact makes it worse, because a wall works in both directions. Your payload is not a passive box being attacked by the cold. It is a box with a 10 W heater bolted inside it.

Modelled with a white EPS box (α ≈ 0.25, ε ≈ 0.85), compact geometry of 0.10 m² — about 20 × 20 × 15 cm — and no radiator. Temperatures at apogee, in °C:

Three things to take from that table

Thicker is worse, not better. Every row is hotter than the one above it. Going from 25 mm to 100 mm costs you about 20 °C on the SoC. Build thick with no heat-rejection path and you have built a very good oven.

The battery column is a safety issue

Those bold numbers — 56, 61, 69, 75 °C — are Li-ion cell temperatures. Li-ion should not be operated above roughly 60 °C; sustained temperatures beyond that degrade the cell and, at the top of that range, risk venting.

This is the one thermal outcome in the whole project that could hurt somebody. It is also the most likely one to be reached, because "insulate thick, then run the LLM hard to show it off" is exactly the natural thing to want to do.

The radiator is not optional if you want to run the LLM continuously. With one, the design becomes insensitive to everything else — thickness, duty cycle, all of it lands in a comfortable band. Without one, your safe envelope is roughly "≤ 30% inference duty, and no thicker than 50 mm".

Keep the thermocol — and put a hole in it

The two are not in conflict. The thick white box insulates against a −55 °C environment and reflects a 1361 W/m² sun; the radiator gives your own waste heat a designed way out.

Minimum viable version: bolt the Pi's heatsink to a 200 × 250 mm sheet of 2 mm aluminium, painted white on the outside, let into one wall of the box so the outer face is exposed. Seal around it with tape. That is an afternoon's work, and it is the difference between the two halves of the table above.

Why white, and not the shiny option

A radiator in sunlight absorbs α × 1361 W/m² and emits ε σ T⁴. You want low solar absorptance and high infrared emittance. SoC temperature at apogee:

This is genuinely counter-intuitive, and an earlier draft of this design specified black anodise before the model was written.

Design posture

Both failure modes are real: over-insulate with no radiator and you cook the Pi; fit 2000 cm² of radiator and the battery reaches −13 °C. There is no number you can look up, because it closes or fails on contact conductance, real emissivity and real dissipation — things you measure.

So build in adjustability: radiator area you can change on the launch field (bolt-on plates, or aluminium tape you can cover part of it with — tape lowers ε, which is exactly the knob you want); the battery strapped to the warm side, never in a corner; and a thermal control loop in flightd that uses inference duty cycle as the actuator. Then let the thermal-vacuum test tell you where to set them.

Structure

  • Frangible on purpose. EPS is cheap, light, insulating and breaks. If this lands on someone, foam is survivable. Never aluminium, wood, or anything with sharp edges or high area density.
  • Load path in tension through the box, never through a glued attachment point.
  • Vent it with a ~3 mm hole. A sealed box at ground pressure will bulge and pop seams at 25 mbar. Add silica gel.
  • Antenna points down, with radials. The null of a ground-plane vertical is along its axis — pointing down puts the null at the balloon and the gain at the horizon and the ground, which is exactly what you want.
  • Bright orange. You will be looking for it in a field.

The exception: Chain 2 must not be a Raspberry Pi

This is the one place I would push back, and it is architectural rather than preferential. Chain 2's entire job is to fail differently from Chain 1. Put a Pi Zero and an SD card in it and you have made the two chains correlated: the same OS, the same storage technology, the same class of software failure.

Three concrete differences:

  • Boot time. An ESP32 is transmitting 200 ms after power-on. A Pi Zero takes 30–60 s to boot Linux. If Chain 2 brownouts during descent you lose a minute of position beacons at the exact moment you need them.
  • No SD card. Filesystem corruption on brownout is failure F4: the most likely way Chain 1 dies. Chain 2 must be immune to it, and bare-metal firmware in flash is.
  • Power. An ESP32 deep-sleeps between beacons at a fraction of a Pi Zero's idle draw. Both fit inside the 4×L91 budget, but Chain 2 has to still be transmitting the morning after a slipped recovery.

So: one Heltec V3 for Chain 2, and HATs everywhere else. That is one ESP32 in the whole project instead of four.

A plain ESP32 has no radio

The ESP-WROOM-32 board at ₹399 is a microcontroller and nothing else. To test LoRa with it you have to add a radio, which means one of:

  • The bare E22: 22 castellated pads to solder, which is the whole thing we sidestepped.
  • Jumper wires to an SX1262 HAT: possible in principle since SPI is SPI, but you would need that HAT's schematic to know which of the 40 header positions carry NSS, BUSY, DIO1, NRST, RXEN and TXEN. We do not have it, and guessing wastes an evening.

The Heltec V3 is an ESP32 with the SX1262 already on it. ₹2,299, and it is already in your BOM for Chain 2. Use it as the second test node during Phase 1: before it takes up its flight role. Nothing to solder, nothing to look up, and an OLED showing live RSSI.

⚠️ If you ever do cut a battery: never cut both wires at once

The adapter above means you should never need to. But the rule matters for the rest of the project, so learn it now: a LiPo has no switch, and putting metal cutters across both leads at once is a dead short across a 2000 mAh cell: a fire, not a spark.

  1. Cut one wire. Strip it, solder it to the matching A1251H wire, and heat-shrink it closed.
  2. Only then cut the second wire and repeat.
  3. At no point should both conductors be bare at the same time.
  4. Check polarity with a multimeter before plugging in. Find the + and − marking next to the Heltec's battery socket, measure your adapter, and confirm they match. Reverse polarity will destroy the board instantly and there is no undo.

  • Solder two wires between the 2.0 mm male header and the 1.25 mm cable, red to red and black to black.

Your first two hours: that workflow is exactly right

Both boards flashed over USB-C from the laptop; one on a battery sending an incrementing count; the other receiving and showing the numbers on its own OLED. No Pi, no wiring, no laptop in the field. Here is the sequence, with the things that trip people up.

  1. Screw the antennas on before applying power. Both boards, every time. Powering a LoRa transmitter into an open connector can destroy its PA, and it is the easiest mistake to make while excited about new hardware.
  2. Toolchain: Arduino IDE, add the ESP32 board package, select Heltec WiFi LoRa 32(V3), install RadioLib, Adafruit SSD1306 and Adafruit GFX. No serial port appearing is nearly always a charge-only USB-C cable.
  3. Flash TX to one, RX to the other, then label them with tape. They are identical boards and you will mix them up on a rooftop.
  4. Bench test at 1 m first. RX should show the count climbing and RSSI around −30 to −40 dBm. Do not skip this: it is the only test where you know the answer in advance, so it is the only one that proves the system works before distance becomes a variable.
  5. Then opposite ends of the building, then the real distances.
⚠️ The power bank will probably switch itself off

This is the one that wastes an afternoon. Most USB power banks shut down when current draw falls below about 50–100 mA. An ESP32 beaconing once a second draws far less than that on average, so the bank decides nothing is plugged in and cuts power.

You leave the transmitter on a rooftop, drive 10 km, and receive nothing, and the fault is neither the radio nor your code.

Fixes, best first: a 3.7 V LiPo on the board's battery connector (SH1.25-2: the Heltec charges it over USB-C, and it is what you fly anyway); a power bank with a low-current / trickle mode; or at minimum test the bank first: plug in the beaconing board, walk away ten minutes, come back and check the OLED is still counting.

Put the OLED on the transmitter too

Your plan has the display on the receiver, which is right, but give the transmitter one as well. Before you leave a board on a rooftop you want to see it is actually transmitting, and when you come back you want to know how many packets it sent. The two counts together give you the true packet error rate rather than an inferred one.

The TX sketch with OLED output is in docs/06-field-tests.md §0.8: it shows the frequency, spreading factor and a large running count, readable at arm's length.

One thing to be clear about: the received count is not the measurement. RSSI is. At every test point record both GPS positions and antenna heights, the true distance computed from them, TX and RX counts, RSSI mean/min/max, SNR, and then the predicted RSSI from tools/link_budget.py and the difference. That last line is the point of the whole exercise; everything else is bookkeeping.

Protocol: run this identically at every site

  1. Antennas vertical at both ends, on tripods, at a recorded height. Tilt one 90° and you lose 20–30 dB to polarisation mismatch.
  2. Record real GPS coordinates and heights. Not "about 3 km": the actual distance computed from the coordinates.
  3. Sweep the settings. For SF7, SF9 and SF12 at 125 kHz: transmit 200 packets at 1 Hz with an incrementing sequence number.
  4. Log at the receiver: sequence, RSSI, SNR, frequency error, time. Missing sequence numbers give you the packet error rate.
  5. Compare measured RSSI against the model and record the delta. That comparison is the entire point of the exercise.

Pass criterion applies only to test 1.4: measured RSSI within 6 dB of tools/link_budget.py. The city tests have no pass criterion: record the delta and explain it with the Fresnel table.

Antenna handling: what you asked about

  • Never power the board without an antenna connected. The PA drives an open circuit and can destroy itself. Screw it on first, every time.
  • Height beats everything. Two metres higher usually helps more than any settings change.
  • Your body is an obstruction: standing in front of the antenna costs several dB. Step away and let it run.
  • Hand-tighten SMA only, finger-tight plus a nudge. Over-tightening ruins the connector.
  • Keep the antenna at least 10 cm clear of metal and of the board itself.

Phase 2 — cold testing at −25 to −30 °C

Not "does it survive the cold": that is too vague to fail. Four specific questions:

  1. Does the LLM still run, and how fast? Log tokens/second at room temperature and at −25 °C.
  2. How far does the radio's frequency drift? The crystal moves with temperature. getFrequencyError() gives you Hz per °C: the number that sizes your flight receiver's AFC window, which is failure F7.
  3. What happens to the battery under load? This is where you find out whether the Li-ion decision was right.
  4. Does anything simply stop? The SD card, the GPS, an I²C sensor. Log at 1 Hz and look for gaps.

Run the complete flight software, not a test script, for four hours minimum: longer than the flight plus pre-launch. Put DS18B20 probes on the SoC heatsink, the battery, the radio and free air. A freezer door will close on a thin coax and power cable without damage.

⚠️ Condensation will destroy the hardware if you get this wrong

Take a −25 °C payload out into a 30 °C Pune afternoon and water condenses on and inside every component instantly: including under chips, where you cannot see it or dry it. Power it up wet and it dies. This kills more test hardware than the cold ever does.

The procedure: before the test, seal the payload in a zip-lock bag with several silica gel sachets and as little air as possible. Run the test sealed. When you take it out, leave it sealed for at least two hours until it is fully back to room temperature: the condensation then forms on the outside of the bag, which is exactly what you want. Only then open it.

The same rule applies on launch day. The payload comes back cold-soaked from 25 km. Do not open it in the field.

Pass: four hours with no gaps in the log, every sensor reporting, battery above cutoff, inference inside the 90 s timeout, drift recorded in Hz/°C, and no damage verified the day after, not the same hour.

Wiring

Path A: the Heltec board

There is no wiring. That is the point. Screw the antenna on first, plug in USB-C, install the Arduino IDE, add the ESP32 board package, select Heltec WiFi LoRa 32(V3), install the RadioLib library, upload the sketch. If the board does not appear as a serial port it is almost always the cable: many USB-C cables carry power only.

Path B: the E22 on an adapter board

Only if you decide you want the 30 dBm PA, and only after Phase 1. An adapter board is a small PCB with the E22's footprint on one side and ordinary 2.54 mm header holes on the other.

Castellated pads are genuinely the friendliest surface-mount package because the pad is a half-hole on the edge: you touch the iron to the side rather than reaching underneath. Iron at 330 °C, 0.6 mm leaded solder, flux pen, tweezers. Tin one corner pad, place the module, re-melt that one pad and nudge it into alignment, let it cool. Check it is straight before doing anything else: while only one pad is soldered you can still fix it. Then solder the opposite corner, then work along each edge one pad at a time. Inspect for bridges, and check continuity with the multimeter: each pad to its header hole near 0 Ω, and no two adjacent pads connected.

Then eleven wires to the ESP32. The most common beginner mistake is wiring only the four SPI lines: BUSY, DIO1 and NRST are required and the radio will not respond without them. The full pin table is in the Parts & wiring section, and the E22 needs its own 5 V supply, never the ESP32's 3.3 V pin.

One gotcha that will cost you an evening

Heltec label DIO1 as "DIO0" in some of their own documentation and headers. GPIO14 is the correct interrupt pin for RadioLib on the V3. Ignore the silkscreen. The board's internal map is NSS 8, DIO1 14, RST 12, BUSY 13, SCK 9, MISO 11, MOSI 10.

Recovery

The mission objective, dressed up as an afterthought in most people's plans.

Terminal search sequence

  1. LTE tracker SMS → coordinates to ~10 m. If this works you are done. This is why Chain 3 is worth ₹3,000.
  2. Last known LoRa position, extrapolated → roughly a 1 km circle.
  3. Drive to the circle and listen on the omni. A payload on the ground at 100 mW is receivable at 1–5 km in open terrain.
  4. Handheld RDF — a 3-element 434 MHz Yagi and a step attenuator. Walk toward increasing signal, adding attenuation as it strengthens. This is the technique that finds it in standing cane when nothing else has.
  5. Drone visual search over the last 200 m.

The drone — an honest assessment

Your instinct is right that a drone helps, but be clear about why, because it changes what you should build.

It does not meaningfully extend your radio horizon. A drone at 120 m has a horizon of ~39 km; horizon-to-horizon with a payload on the ground is ~44 km. But you will already be within a couple of kilometres before you launch it, so horizon was never the binding constraint.

What it actually buys you is real and worth having: clearing terrain masking, where 120 m of altitude is the difference between no signal and a solid lock; a visual search platform that covers a cane field in minutes rather than an hour on foot; and reach across a canal or a crop you should not trample.

Regulatory reality

DGCA requires VLOS and ≤ 120 m AGL, in a Green Zone, with a registered drone and a certified remote pilot. You cannot fly the drone toward the payload beyond your own visual line of sight.

So the concept of operations is not "chase the payload with a drone". It is: drive to within ~1 km using the LTE tracker and the last LoRa fix, then launch the drone to see over the terrain and find it visually.

Recommended drone payload: the simplest thing that works — a 40 g ESP32 + SX1262 that logs (time, lat, lon, alt, RSSI, SNR) for every payload beacon it hears. Do not build a complex airborne repeater. You want fewer failure modes, not more.

Not causing an incident

An unmarked foam box with wires and antennas, found in a field by a farmer, is a bomb scare, a police case, and possibly the end of your project. This has happened to ballooning teams around the world. It is not a hypothetical.

Mandatory

Bright orange or pink. No visible wires. Nothing black, nothing that looks military. Consider a translucent container so the contents are visibly benign.

A printed label on every face, in English, Hindi and Marathi:

HARMLESS SCIENTIFIC WEATHER EXPERIMENT — NOT DANGEROUS
This is a student research atmospheric balloon payload. It contains a small computer, a camera and a radio. There are no explosives, no chemicals and no hazardous material. Please do not open. Please call +91-XXXXXXXXXX — a reward will be given.
Boscon Space · launch date · DGCA/ATC reference number

Pre-notify the police stations in every district along your predicted track, with the launch time, a photo and your number. Carry printed permissions in the chase car.

Language choice: Python is fine for flightd, sensord and camd — the libraries are all there and you will iterate fast. Write radiod in Rust or Go: it owns the only link you have, it carries hard duty-cycle obligations, and it must not be the thing that raises an unhandled exception at 25 km.

Six rules that are not negotiable

  1. Every process restarts cleanly from disk state. No in-memory-only state. A crash anywhere loses at most the current cycle. Restart=always.
  2. The beacon path has no dependencies. radiod must emit a position beacon even if flightd, llmd and camd are all dead — it reads last-known position from a file that sensord writes.
  3. Log everything, locally, always. Radio is a bonus. The NDJSON log on the NVMe is your real dataset. 1 Hz minimum, fsynced.
  4. No network, no DNS, no NTP in flight. Time comes from GPS. Disable WiFi and Bluetooth at boot — they are also RF noise sources sitting next to your receiver.
  5. Feature-flag everything so you can disable a misbehaving subsystem over the uplink.
  6. Deterministic replay. The flight log must be sufficient to replay the whole mission through your SIL harness. That is how you debug flight 1 into flight 2.

Frame formats

Downlink beacon (SF9/250k, every 20 s, 32 bytes)
  u8  magic=0xB5    u8  type=0x01     u16 seq
  i32 lat_e7        i32 lon_e7        i32 alt_mm
  i16 temp_ext_c10  i16 temp_soc_c10  u16 pressure_pa10
  u8  state         u8  batt_pct      u8  flags
  u16 crc16

Uplink command (SF9/125k, variable)
  u8  magic=0xB5    u8  type=0x10     u32 counter   # monotonic, anti-replay
  u8  len           u8  payload[len]
  u8  hmac[4]                                       # HMAC-SHA256(key, above)[0:4]

Downlink data chunk (SF7/250k, <= 64 bytes)
  u8  magic=0xB5    u8  type=0x20     u16 msg_id
  u8  chunk_idx     u8  chunk_total   u8  len
  u8  data[len]     u16 crc16

Ground reassembles by msg_id and requests missing chunks with a selective NACK. The response is signed as a whole message, so a tampered or partial reassembly is detectable.

Future: 100 Mbps and a mesh

Forward planning only — none of this is in scope for HAB-1. Two questions are tangled together in the brief, and they have very different answers.

What 100 Mbps actually costs

One equation you cannot argue with: C = B·log₂(1 + SNR). Power buys you the logarithm; bandwidth buys you the multiplier.

Spectral efficiencyModulationSNR neededBandwidth for 100 Mbps
1 b/s/HzBPSK 1/25.0 dB100 MHz

4 b/s/Hz16QAM 3/416.8 dB25 MHz
5 b/s/Hz32APSK 5/619.9 dB20 MHz
This is arithmetic, not engineering

Even at a demanding 5 bits/s/Hz you need 20 MHz of clean spectrum. India's entire 865–867 MHz allocation is 2 MHz wide. Its Shannon ceiling at a generous 10 dB SNR is 6.9 Mbps — and the 1% duty cycle then divides that by a hundred.

No amount of power, coding or cleverness reaches 100 Mbps in sub-GHz ISM. The first decision is a band change, and everything else follows from it.

Band selection — and why higher is better up there

Path loss rises with frequency, but antenna gain for a fixed aperture also rises with frequency — and the aperture term wins twice, once at each end. With 30 cm at both ends, 1 W, over a 100 km hop:

2.4 GHz15.0 dBi29.1°11.8 dB161 Mbps
5.8 GHz22.6 dBi12.1°19.5 dB260 Mbps
10 GHz27.4 dBi7.0°24.2 dB322 Mbps
24 GHz35.0 dBi2.9°31.8 dB423 Mbps

And at 25 km you are above the weather that normally makes high frequencies painful — no rain fade, no cloud, negligible water vapour. Bands that are marginal at sea level are excellent up there. That is the single strongest reason the stratosphere is a good place to do high-rate radio.

Max hop length at a full 100 Mbps, 40 MHz channel, 16QAM 3/4, 1 W, 6 dB margin: a 30 cm dish at 5.8 GHz reaches 123 km; 20 cm still gives 55 km. The link budget closes easily. Reach was never the problem.

The actual problem: pointing

Every dB of gain narrows the beam — and a HAB payload spins at 1–5 rpm and swings ±10–20°. Pointing loss = 12(error/beamwidth)² dB:

Three ways out: stay wide (10 cm at 2.4 GHz gives an 87° beam that survives any attitude, capping you at 20–40 km per hop — and needs no new subsystem at all); a 2-axis gimbal driven by an IMU and the neighbour's GPS position, good to ~1°; or a phased array.

The mesh: what it actually buys you

The brief says a distant balloon has "no elevation" to the ground station. That is worth checking, because it changes the design.

Three things the mesh actually buys: only one is in the brief

1. Rate, not reach. A single 500 km link cannot run 100 Mbps on a small aperture; five 100 km hops can. The mesh exists to shorten hops so each can run fast. That is the real argument, and it is a good one.

2. The descent blackout, and this one matters today. The first genuinely useful mesh application is not 100 Mbps at all. It is a second balloon relaying LoRa position packets from a descending payload your ground station can no longer hear: the exact gap in HAB-1's comms analysis. It needs no new radio, no pointing and no new vehicle. It is achievable on HAB-2 with hardware already on the parts list.

3. Reach, but only once you can float. The premise becomes true past ~576 km of drift, which a two-hour burst flight never reaches.

The real gate is the vehicle, not the radio

A latex balloon bursts after 90 minutes having drifted ~112 km. A mesh of burst balloons is a two-hour mesh: all your nodes launch together, drift together, land within 40 minutes of each other, and stay inside one horizon throughout. The topology barely changes.

The mesh only becomes interesting when nodes can float and separate. Loon flew super-pressure balloons for 100+ days, steering by changing altitude to catch different wind layers. That is a different vehicle class, and a far harder regulatory ask: it stays up, crosses FIRs, and possibly borders.

So: solve the vehicle before the radio. The radio is well understood and buyable. A float-capable balloon that Indian regulators will let you keep aloft is the slow part.

Architecture: two planes

CONTROL PLANE: LoRa 868 MHz, omnidirectional, always on, ~1 kbps
    every node broadcasts position, altitude, attitude, battery, link state
    so every node knows where every other node is
    carries routing, link scheduling, commands, and the beacon of last resort

DATA PLANE: 5.8 GHz (later optical), directional, opportunistic, 10-100+ Mbps
    established only when the control plane says two nodes are in range and aligned
    ACM rides the geometry down: 100 -> 10 -> 1 Mbps -> fall back to control plane
    carries bulk science data, images, video

The control plane solves the data plane's acquisition problem. This is the key idea. A 12° beam cannot find another balloon by searching — but if you already know its GPS position to 2 m and your own attitude to 1°, you point straight at it. The cheap omni link is what makes the fragile directional link possible.

Routing: topology is predictable, not discovered. Use Contact Graph Routing over predicted positions and Bundle Protocol (RFC 9171) for store-and-forward: what NASA's DTN does for spacecraft. Do not reach for AODV or OLSR; they assume discovery and churn, and you have neither.

Six things to decide in HAB-1 that keep these doors open

All cost nothing now and are expensive to retrofit.

  1. Put a node ID and hop count in the LoRa frame today. Two spare bytes. Without them every frame is implicitly point-to-point and the whole protocol needs redesigning for HAB-2.
  2. Make the radio layer address-based: send(to=node_id), not send(). Even with one node, write it as a network.
  3. Log the full attitude solution: IMU, magnetometer, GPS. You cannot size a gimbal until you know how much a real payload actually spins and swings, and HAB-1 is your only free chance to measure it. This is the argument for promoting the IMU off the DEFER list.
  4. Log RSSI and SNR on every packet at both ends. This is the dataset that calibrates every future link budget.
  5. Keep mass and power margins honest. A 5.8 GHz radio plus gimbal is ~1 kg and ~10 W; HAB-1's real margins tell you what HAB-2 can carry.
  6. Start the ASOC licence now. The EIRP ceilings that constrain HAB-1 get worse at high rate: a 22.6 dBi dish at 1 W is +52 dBm EIRP, far outside any licence-exempt allowance. Amateur allocations at 2.4, 5.65–5.85, 10 and 24 GHz permit far higher power. For this programme the licence stops being an optimisation and becomes the enabling condition.

Worth noting: free-space optical needs no spectrum licence at all. For a project that will otherwise spend years negotiating for bandwidth, that is a strategic argument for optical quite separate from its performance.
