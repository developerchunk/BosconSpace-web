Assembly & setup · from scratch

Two radios,
one working link

Everything from opening the boxes to a packet arriving 20 km away. Written assuming you have never soldered, flashed a microcontroller, or connected an antenna. Follow it in order — every step ends with a checkpoint that tells you whether it worked.

STEP 00

Know what you are holding

Six line items arrive in two parcels. Before touching anything, lay them out on a table and match each one to this list. If a bag does not match its description, stop and check the order — fitting the wrong connector is how boards get damaged.

Read this before you plug anything in

Never power a LoRa board without its antenna attached. A transmitter with nothing to radiate into reflects its own power back into the output stage. At the 22 dBm (160 mW) setting used here that can permanently degrade or kill the SX1262. This is the single most common way beginners destroy a LoRa board — and it is silent, so you will not know until range is mysteriously terrible.

Rule for the rest of your life with these boards: antenna first, power second, every single time.

What you also need, which you already own

  • A laptop with a free USB port and internet access, for installing the software and flashing the boards.
  • Two USB-C cables that carry data, not just charge. Cables bundled with phone chargers are often charge-only and will make the board appear dead to your laptop. A cable that came with a hard drive, headset or another dev board is a safe bet. If you own exactly one known-good cable, you can do everything one board at a time.
  • Masking tape and a pen, to label the boards. Do not skip this — the two boards are identical and you will confuse them.
  • Optional: a phone with a compass and a GPS app for the field tests.
Checkpoint 00

Every one of the six items is on the table and you can name it without looking at the list. You have located the gold 2 mm circle on each Heltec and you understand it is a different thing from the metal spring.

STEP 01

Fit the antennas

This is the most delicate operation in the whole build and it comes first, because nothing else may happen until the antennas are on. The u.FL connector is rated for roughly 10 mating cycles in its whole life — treat every connection as precious. Do this on a clear table in good light, sitting down, with the board flat.

1a · Seat the u.FL plug on the board

  1. Put the Heltec on the table, screen up, and find the small gold circle at the board edge. It has a raised gold rim with a pinpoint in the centre. Do not confuse it with the silver spring soldered flat to the board.
  2. Hold the pigtail's u.FL end between finger and thumb. Look at it from the side: it is a little cup that has to sit down over the rim on the board.
  3. Rest the cup on the socket and rock it very slightly until you feel it drop and sit flat. It must be concentric — perfectly centred. If it is even slightly off-centre, pressing will bend the rim and ruin the socket permanently.
  4. With it sitting flat and centred, press straight down with your fingernail or the flat back of a plastic pen cap. You will feel and usually hear a small click. That click is the whole game.
  5. Tug the coax gently sideways. It should not lift. If it pops off, it was never seated — re-centre and try again.
If you ever need to take it off again

Never pull the cable. Pulling the coax lifts the socket clean off the circuit board and the board is then finished. Slide a fingernail or a thin flat plastic edge under the metal cup itself, on opposite sides, and lever it straight up. Better still: once it is on, leave it on for the whole test campaign.

1b · Screw on the antenna

  1. Take the SMA-female end of the pigtail (the one with a hole) in one hand and the antenna (the one with a pin) in the other.
  2. Push them together straight and turn the outer knurled barrel clockwise. Never twist the antenna body against the cable — that twists the coax and can break the inner conductor.
  3. Turn until it is firm with fingers only. Do not use pliers. Over-torquing an SMA crushes the dielectric and detunes the connection.
  4. Fold the antenna up to 90° if the hinge allows, so it stands vertical when the board lies flat. LoRa antennas are vertically polarised — both ends must be vertical or you throw away roughly 20 dB.

1c · Repeat for board two

Do the identical thing on the second board, then put a strip of masking tape on each and write TX on one and RX on the other. You will thank yourself in Step 5.

Checkpoint 01

Both boards have a coax firmly clicked to the gold socket, an antenna finger-tight on the other end of that coax, and a tape label. A gentle sideways tug on either coax does not dislodge it. Neither board has been plugged into anything yet.

STEP 02

First power-up — is the board alive?

Before you install a single piece of software, prove the hardware works. This takes two minutes and saves you from spending an evening debugging a toolchain when the real problem was a charge-only cable.

  1. Confirm the antenna is on. (Yes, again. Every time.)
  2. Plug the USB-C cable into the board, then into your laptop.
  3. Look at the board. A small power LED should light. The OLED usually runs the factory demo firmware — a Heltec logo, a WiFi scan, or scrolling text. What it shows does not matter; that it shows anything is the point.
  4. Check that the laptop can see it. On macOS open Terminal and run the command below; on Windows open Device Manager and look under Ports (COM & LPT).
ls /dev/cu.*

You are looking for a new entry that was not there before you plugged in. It will be named something like /dev/cu.usbserial-0001, /dev/cu.SLAB_USBtoUART or /dev/cu.usbmodem…. On Windows it will be COM3, COM5 or similar. Write down the exact name — you need it in Step 4.

If no port appears

Work through these in order — the first one is the cause about 80% of the time.

  • Charge-only cable. Swap to a different USB-C cable, ideally one that came with a data device. This is by far the most common cause.
  • Missing USB-serial driver. These boards use a Silicon Labs CP2102 bridge. macOS 11 and later has it built in; on Windows or older macOS, install the CP210x USB to UART Bridge VCP Driver from the Silicon Labs site, then re-plug.
  • USB hub. Plug directly into the laptop, not through a dock or hub.
  • Dead board. Only conclude this after the other three. Try the second board with the same cable — if that one enumerates, you have a hardware fault to report to Robocraze.

Now repeat for the second board so you know both are healthy before you write any code. Then unplug both.

Checkpoint 02

Both boards light up, and each one makes a named serial port appear on your laptop when plugged in and disappear when unplugged. You have both port names written down.

STEP 03

Install the software on your laptop

You need three things: the Arduino IDE (the editor that compiles and uploads), the ESP32 board support package (which teaches it about this chip), and three libraries (radio and screen drivers). Everything here is free and this is a one-time setup.

3a · Arduino IDE

  1. Go to arduino.cc/en/software and download Arduino IDE 2.x for your operating system.
  2. Install and open it. On macOS you may have to right-click → Open the first time because it is downloaded from the internet.

3b · Teach it about the ESP32-S3

  1. Open Settings — on macOS Arduino IDE → Settings…, on Windows File → Preferences.
  2. Find the field labelled Additional boards manager URLs and paste the URL below into it. If there is already something there, add a comma first.
  3. Click OK.
  4. Open Tools → Board → Boards Manager… (or the second icon down the left rail). Type esp32 in the search box.
  5. Find esp32 by Espressif Systems and click Install. It is a large download — expect several minutes on a normal connection. Let it finish completely.
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/espressif/arduino-esp32/gh-pages/package_esp32_index.json

3c · Install the three libraries

  1. Open Tools → Manage Libraries… (or the books icon in the left rail).
  2. Search RadioLib and install RadioLib by Jan Gromes. This is the driver for the SX1262 radio and it is the reason we do not have to write register-level code.
  3. Search Adafruit SSD1306 and install Adafruit SSD1306. When it offers to install dependencies, click Install All.
  4. If it did not come in automatically, search Adafruit GFX and install Adafruit GFX Library too.

3d · Select the board

  1. Go to Tools → Board → esp32 and choose Heltec WiFi LoRa 32(V3). Read it carefully — there are entries for V1 and V2 in the same list and picking the wrong one gives you a board that compiles, uploads, and then does nothing, with no error to tell you why.
  2. Leave every other setting at its default. Upload speed 921600 is fine; drop it to 115200 later if uploads prove flaky.
Checkpoint 03

The Arduino IDE window shows Heltec WiFi LoRa 32(V3) in the board selector at the top, and RadioLib, Adafruit SSD1306 and Adafruit GFX all appear as Installed in the Library Manager.

STEP 04

First upload — prove the pipe before you use it

Do not jump straight to radio code. Upload a sketch that only lights the screen. If it works, you know the cable, the driver, the board selection, the compiler and the uploader are all correct — so when the radio sketch misbehaves later you know the fault is in the radio, not in five other things at once.

The gotcha that catches everyone

On the Heltec V3 the OLED is not permanently powered. It is fed through a switch controlled by GPIO36, called Vext, and that switch is active LOW — you must drive the pin LOW to turn the screen on. Leave it out and the screen stays dark forever while your code runs perfectly. Every sketch on this page handles it in the first lines of setup().

  1. In the Arduino IDE, File → New Sketch. Delete everything in the window and paste the code below.
  2. Save it as 00_hello.
  3. Plug in one board (antenna on). Choose its port under Tools → Port — the name you wrote down in Step 2.
  4. Press the → (Upload) arrow at the top left. First compile takes a minute or two; later ones are quick.
  5. Watch the black console at the bottom. You want to see Connecting.... then Writing at 0x… then Hard resetting via RTS pin...
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_GFX.h>
#include <Adafruit_SSD1306.h>

// ---- Heltec WiFi LoRa 32 V3 display pins ----
#define OLED_SDA 17
#define OLED_SCL 18
#define OLED_RST 21
#define VEXT     36     // powers the OLED rail. ACTIVE LOW.

Adafruit_SSD1306 display(128, 64, &Wire, OLED_RST);

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(300);
  Serial.println("boot ok");

  pinMode(VEXT, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(VEXT, LOW);          // LOW = screen powered
  delay(50);

  pinMode(OLED_RST, OUTPUT);        // pulse the display reset line
  digitalWrite(OLED_RST, LOW);  delay(20);
  digitalWrite(OLED_RST, HIGH); delay(20);

  Wire.begin(OLED_SDA, OLED_SCL);
  if (!display.begin(SSD1306_SWITCHCAPVCC, 0x3C)) {
    Serial.println("OLED not found at 0x3C");
    while (true) delay(1000);
  }

  display.clearDisplay();
  display.setTextColor(SSD1306_WHITE);
  display.setTextSize(1);
  display.setCursor(0, 0);
  display.println("HAB-1 PHASE 1");
  display.println("");
  display.println("board alive");
  display.display();
}

void loop() {
  Serial.println("alive");
  delay(1000);
}

Now open Tools → Serial Monitor and set the baud rate selector on the right to 115200. If it is at any other value you will see garbage characters — that is not a fault, it is the wrong speed.

If the upload fails with a connection or timeout error

The ESP32 has to be talked into its download mode. Normally the board does this on its own, but if it will not:

  1. Hold down the BOOT button (sometimes marked PRG or USER).
  2. While holding it, briefly press and release RST (RESET).
  3. Release BOOT.
  4. Press Upload in the IDE immediately.

If it still fails, drop Tools → Upload Speed to 115200 and try again. After a manual-mode upload you must press RST once for the new program to actually start running.

When it works, do the identical thing on the second board. Both must reach this checkpoint before you move on.

Checkpoint 04

Both boards display HAB-1 PHASE 1 / board alive on their OLEDs, and the Serial Monitor at 115200 prints alive once a second. You know how to select a port, upload, and open the serial monitor without looking it up.

STEP 05

Flash the transmitter and the receiver

Two sketches. One board counts and sends; the other listens and shows what arrived, plus how strong it was. The signal-strength numbers are the entire point of Phase 1 — they are what tells you how far you can go before the link dies.

Frequency — use 866.1 MHz, not 868.1

868.1 MHz is the European channel and it is what most tutorials online use. In India the licence-exempt band is 865–867 MHz, so 868.1 MHz is outside it. Both sketches below are set to 866.1 MHz, which sits comfortably inside the Indian allocation. Your 863–928 MHz boards cover it fine. If you copy code from elsewhere, change the frequency before you transmit.

The pin map, once, for reference

These numbers are why a generic LoRa tutorial will not work on this board. They are already filled into both sketches — you do not need to change anything.

SignalGPIOWhat it does
NSS / CS8Chip select for the radio
SCK9SPI clock
MOSI10Data to the radio
MISO11Data from the radio
NRST12Radio reset
BUSY13Radio says “wait, I'm working”
DIO114Radio interrupt — “done” / “packet arrived”

Ignore the silkscreen here

Heltec's own board printing labels the interrupt pin DIO0. On an SX1262 the pin that matters is DIO1, and it is on GPIO14. The printing on the board is misleading; the table above is right. If you ever see a sketch using GPIO26 or DIO0 by name, it was written for the V2 board, which used a completely different radio chip.

5a · The transmitter

Plug in the board you labelled TX, select its port, paste this, save it as 01_tx, upload.

#include <RadioLib.h>
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_GFX.h>
#include <Adafruit_SSD1306.h>

// ---------- PICK ONE MODE. Must be the SAME on both boards. ----------
#define USE_FSK    1         // 1 = GFSK 38.4 kb/s   0 = LoRa SF9

// ---------- radio config: keep IDENTICAL on both boards ----------
#define FREQ_MHZ   866.1     // India 865-868 MHz band
#define TX_DBM     22        // 160 mW e.r.p. - inside Table II's 500 mW limit

#if USE_FSK
  #define BITRATE_K   38.4   // kb/s  -- 7.7x faster than LoRa SF9
  #define FDEV_K      40.0   // kHz frequency deviation
  #define RXBW_K     156.2   // kHz receiver filter. Occupied BW stays under
                             // 200 kHz, which is the G.S.R. 853(E) Table II cap.
  #define SEND_EVERY_MS 1000 // ~5 ms airtime -> 0.5% duty. Legal limit is 2.5%.
#else
  #define BW_KHZ     125.0
  #define SF         9
  #define CR         5       // 4/5
  #define SYNC_WORD  0x12    // private network
  #define SEND_EVERY_MS 8000 // 185 ms airtime -> 2.3% duty. Legal limit is 2.5%.
#endif

// ---------- Heltec V3 wiring ----------
#define OLED_SDA 17
#define OLED_SCL 18
#define OLED_RST 21
#define VEXT     36

SPIClass       spiLoRa(HSPI);
SPISettings    spiCfg(2000000, MSBFIRST, SPI_MODE0);
//                    NSS DIO1 NRST BUSY
SX1262 radio = new Module(8, 14, 12, 13, spiLoRa, spiCfg);

Adafruit_SSD1306 display(128, 64, &Wire, OLED_RST);
uint32_t counter = 0;

void screen(const String &a, const String &b, const String &c) {
  display.clearDisplay();
  display.setCursor(0, 0);
  display.println(a); display.println(b); display.println(c);
  display.display();
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(300);

  pinMode(VEXT, OUTPUT);  digitalWrite(VEXT, LOW);  delay(50);
  pinMode(OLED_RST, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(OLED_RST, LOW); delay(20); digitalWrite(OLED_RST, HIGH); delay(20);
  Wire.begin(OLED_SDA, OLED_SCL);
  display.begin(SSD1306_SWITCHCAPVCC, 0x3C);
  display.setTextColor(SSD1306_WHITE);
  display.setTextSize(1);

  spiLoRa.begin(9, 11, 10, 8);          // SCK, MISO, MOSI, NSS

#if USE_FSK
  int st = radio.beginFSK(FREQ_MHZ, BITRATE_K, FDEV_K, RXBW_K, TX_DBM);
  if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    uint8_t sync[] = {0x2D, 0xD4};   // FSK sync word, both boards identical
    radio.setSyncWord(sync, 2);
    radio.setCRC(2);                 // 2-byte CRC
  }
#else
  int st = radio.begin(FREQ_MHZ, BW_KHZ, SF, CR, SYNC_WORD, TX_DBM);
#endif
  if (st != RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    Serial.printf("radio.begin failed: %d\n", st);
    screen("TX", "RADIO FAIL", String(st));
    while (true) delay(1000);
  }
  Serial.println("TX ready");
  screen("HAB-1 TX", USE_FSK ? "866.1 FSK38k" : "866.1 SF9", "ready");
}

void loop() {
  counter++;
  String msg = "HAB1 " + String(counter);

  uint32_t t0 = millis();
  int st = radio.transmit(msg);
  uint32_t air = millis() - t0;

  if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    Serial.printf("sent #%lu  air=%lums\n", counter, air);
    screen("HAB-1 TX", "sent #" + String(counter), String(air) + " ms air");
  } else {
    Serial.printf("tx error %d\n", st);
    screen("HAB-1 TX", "TX ERR " + String(st), "");
  }
  delay(SEND_EVERY_MS);
}

5b · The receiver

Unplug TX. Plug in RX, re-select the port (it may have a different name), paste this, save as 02_rx, upload.

#include <RadioLib.h>
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_GFX.h>
#include <Adafruit_SSD1306.h>

// ---------- PICK ONE MODE. Must be the SAME on both boards. ----------
#define USE_FSK    1         // 1 = GFSK 38.4 kb/s   0 = LoRa SF9

// ---------- radio config: keep IDENTICAL on both boards ----------
#define FREQ_MHZ   866.1     // India 865-868 MHz band
#define TX_DBM     22        // 160 mW e.r.p. - inside Table II's 500 mW limit

#if USE_FSK
  #define BITRATE_K   38.4   // kb/s  -- 7.7x faster than LoRa SF9
  #define FDEV_K      40.0   // kHz frequency deviation
  #define RXBW_K     156.2   // kHz receiver filter. Occupied BW stays under
                             // 200 kHz, which is the G.S.R. 853(E) Table II cap.
  #define SEND_EVERY_MS 1000 // ~5 ms airtime -> 0.5% duty. Legal limit is 2.5%.
#else
  #define BW_KHZ     125.0
  #define SF         9
  #define CR         5       // 4/5
  #define SYNC_WORD  0x12    // private network
  #define SEND_EVERY_MS 8000 // 185 ms airtime -> 2.3% duty. Legal limit is 2.5%.
#endif

#define OLED_SDA 17
#define OLED_SCL 18
#define OLED_RST 21
#define VEXT     36

SPIClass    spiLoRa(HSPI);
SPISettings spiCfg(2000000, MSBFIRST, SPI_MODE0);
SX1262 radio = new Module(8, 14, 12, 13, spiLoRa, spiCfg);

Adafruit_SSD1306 display(128, 64, &Wire, OLED_RST);
uint32_t got = 0, missed = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  delay(300);

  pinMode(VEXT, OUTPUT);  digitalWrite(VEXT, LOW);  delay(50);
  pinMode(OLED_RST, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(OLED_RST, LOW); delay(20); digitalWrite(OLED_RST, HIGH); delay(20);
  Wire.begin(OLED_SDA, OLED_SCL);
  display.begin(SSD1306_SWITCHCAPVCC, 0x3C);
  display.setTextColor(SSD1306_WHITE);
  display.setTextSize(1);

  spiLoRa.begin(9, 11, 10, 8);

#if USE_FSK
  int st = radio.beginFSK(FREQ_MHZ, BITRATE_K, FDEV_K, RXBW_K, TX_DBM);
  if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    uint8_t sync[] = {0x2D, 0xD4};   // FSK sync word, both boards identical
    radio.setSyncWord(sync, 2);
    radio.setCRC(2);                 // 2-byte CRC
  }
#else
  int st = radio.begin(FREQ_MHZ, BW_KHZ, SF, CR, SYNC_WORD, TX_DBM);
#endif
  if (st != RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    Serial.printf("radio.begin failed: %d\n", st);
    display.clearDisplay(); display.setCursor(0,0);
    display.println("RX"); display.println("RADIO FAIL");
    display.println(String(st)); display.display();
    while (true) delay(1000);
  }
  Serial.println("RX listening");
  display.clearDisplay(); display.setCursor(0,0);
  display.println("HAB-1 RX");
  display.println(USE_FSK ? "866.1 FSK38k" : "866.1 SF9");
  display.println("listening...");
  display.display();
}

void loop() {
  String msg;
  int st = radio.receive(msg);              // blocks, then times out

  if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_NONE) {
    got++;
    float rssi = radio.getRSSI();           // dBm - less negative is better
    float snr  = radio.getSNR();            // dB  - above 0 is comfortable

    Serial.printf("%s | RSSI %.1f dBm | SNR %.1f dB | ok=%lu miss=%lu\n",
                  msg.c_str(), rssi, snr, got, missed);

    display.clearDisplay();
    display.setCursor(0, 0);
    display.setTextSize(2);
    display.println(msg);                   // big: the packet itself
    display.setTextSize(1);
    display.println("");
    display.printf("RSSI %.0f dBm\n", rssi);
    display.printf("SNR  %.1f dB\n", snr);
    display.printf("ok %lu / miss %lu", got, missed);
    display.display();

  } else if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_RX_TIMEOUT) {
    // nothing heard this window - normal between packets
  } else if (st == RADIOLIB_ERR_CRC_MISMATCH) {
    missed++;                               // heard something, too corrupt to use
    Serial.println("CRC fail - edge of range");
  }
}
The duty cycle, and why it is no longer the problem it was

India's rule is G.S.R. 853(E) of 2021. A balloon telemetry beacon is a Table II device (Tracking, Tracing and Data Acquisition — the table's own note lists “Sensors (… meteorology …)” and “Data acquisition”). That gives you 500 mW e.r.p., a 2.5% duty cycle, and 200 kHz of bandwidth — not the 1% that applies to non-specific short-range devices.

ModeAirtimeIntervalDutyHeadroom
LoRa SF9 @ 125 kHz185 ms8 s2.3%tight
GFSK 38.4 kb/s~5 ms1 s0.5%5× spare

At 38.4 kb/s the packet is so short that duty cycle stops being a design constraint. One update per second is legal and makes walking or driving tests far more useful than the 8-second LoRa interval.

Your 22 dBm into a 2.2 dBi antenna is 160 mW e.r.p., comfortably inside Table II's 500 mW. ⚠ If you ever transmit from the ground through the 12 dBi Yagi that becomes 1.5 W e.r.p. — over the limit. Receiving through it is unrestricted; e.r.p. applies only to transmission.

Checkpoint 05

Both uploads completed without error. TX shows HAB-1 TX / ready and RX shows HAB-1 RX / listening.... Neither shows RADIO FAIL — if either does, see Troubleshooting; a non-zero error code there almost always means SPI wiring, which on this board means you picked the wrong board type in Step 3d.

STEP 06

The bench test — your first link

Two boards, one table, five minutes. This is the moment the project stops being a shopping list. It also teaches you to read RSSI and SNR, which is the only skill Phase 1 actually exists to build.

Turn the power down for this test

22 dBm into an antenna two metres away is a very loud shout into a very close ear. The receiver will not be damaged, but its RSSI reading saturates and becomes meaningless, and you learn nothing. For the bench test only, change TX_DBM to 2 in the TX sketch and re-upload. Set it back to 22 before you take the boards outdoors.

  1. Set TX_DBM to 2 in 01_tx and upload. Leave SEND_EVERY_MS alone — at FSK it is already 1 s.
  2. Leave the RX board on 22 — the receiver's power setting is irrelevant when it is only listening.
  3. Power both boards over USB, roughly two metres apart, with both antennas standing vertical and nothing metal between them.
  4. Watch the RX screen.
Checkpoint 06 — the one that matters

Within a few seconds the RX screen shows a large HAB1 1, then HAB1 2, then HAB1 3… counting up in step with the TX board, with an RSSI figure somewhere around −30 to −45 dBm and an SNR comfortably above +8 dB. The miss counter stays at zero.

That is a working LoRa link. Everything after this is the same link, further away.

How to read the two numbers

These are the instruments you will fly the rest of the campaign on. Learn them here where everything is easy.

RSSIMeaningWhat to do
−30 to −60 dBmVery strong. Close range or excellent line of sight.Move further apart — you are learning nothing here.
−60 to −95 dBmComfortable working link.Note the distance. This is what a good flight link looks like.
−95 to −115 dBmWorking, but margin is shrinking.Record it. This is where obstruction starts to bite.
−115 to −129 dBmThe ragged edge. Occasional CRC failures.You have found your range limit for this geometry.
below −129 dBmBelow the theoretical sensitivity of SF9 at 125 kHz. Nothing decodes.Raise the antenna, or raise the spreading factor.

SNR is the more interesting number. LoRa can decode signals buried underneath the noise — at SF9 it works down to about −12.5 dB, meaning the signal can be 12.5 dB weaker than the noise around it and still come through. When you see SNR going negative in the field, you are not failing; you are watching LoRa do the thing it was invented for. When SNR drops below about −10 dB, expect losses and switch to a higher spreading factor.

Prove it to yourself before you go outside

Two experiments, two minutes, and they will save you hours of confusion in the field:

  • Lay one antenna flat while the other stays vertical. Watch RSSI collapse by 15–20 dB. That is polarisation mismatch, and it is the single most common reason a field test underperforms — someone put the transmitter in a bag on its side.
  • Put your body between them. Watch RSSI drop several dB. You are mostly water; so is a hill, a wall, and a crowd. This is why Step 8 is about height, not distance.
STEP 07

Battery — making the transmitter portable

The receiver stays with you and your laptop, so it lives on USB. The transmitter goes up a building or across a valley, so it needs the LiPo. This is the only step with any physical risk in it, so it gets the most warnings — but the actual work is small.

Three rules, no exceptions
  1. Never cut the battery's own wires. Not one at a time, not carefully. If your cutters bridge red and black for even an instant you have shorted a 2000 mAh lithium cell through a pair of pliers. That is a fire on your table. Everything below is designed so the battery's wires are never touched.
  2. Never plug it in without checking polarity first. Reversed polarity into the Heltec's battery input destroys the board instantly and silently.
  3. Never leave a LiPo charging unattended, and never charge a pouch that is swollen, punctured, or has been dropped hard. Charge on a hard, non-flammable surface.

7a · Which case are you in?

Take one NOVA battery and one A1251H lead and hold their two white plugs side by side. You are comparing the spacing between the two metal contacts.

What you seeCaseWhat to do
The battery's plug is the same size as the A1251H plug — both small, contacts ~1.25 mm apart. A The battery plugs straight into the board. Skip to 7c and check polarity. No soldering at all.
The battery's plug is visibly bigger — contacts roughly 2.0 mm apart, plug noticeably wider. B This is what the five WAFER-PH2.0 headers are for. Go to 7b. Four solder joints, which the repair shop does in ten minutes.
You genuinely cannot tell. ? Take a photo with your phone's macro mode and zoom in, or just hand both parts to the repair shop in 7b — they identify connectors all day.

7b · Case B: the adapter

The whole trick is that the battery's plug is a female housing. A male header of the same pitch pushes into it — so you build a short adapter that ends in a male header, and the battery simply plugs onto it. Its own wires are never modified.

What to tell the repair shop

Go to a mobile or TV repair shop — not a house electrician. A house electrician's iron is a 100 W broad tip meant for mains wiring; it will melt these connector housings into slag before the solder flows. A phone repair shop has a fine-tip temperature- controlled iron and does joints this size every day. Hand them the parts and say:

“Please solder the red wire of this small lead to one pin of this 2-pin header, and the black wire to the other pin. Keep red and black on the same sides throughout. Please put heat-shrink or tape over each joint so they cannot touch each other. Do not cut or touch the battery — it is only here so you can check the plug fits.”

Ask them to do two adapters while you are there — one per board, plus you still have three spare headers and three spare leads. It should cost very little; four joints is not meaningfully more work than two.

7c · The polarity check — do not skip this

  1. Look at the Heltec board next to its battery socket. There is printing on the board — either + and −, or VBAT and GND. It is small; photograph it with your phone's macro mode and zoom in rather than squinting.
  2. Hold the A1251H plug next to the socket in the orientation it would go in, and trace which wire lands on which marking.
  3. Red must land on + (VBAT). Black must land on − (GND).
  4. If they come out the other way round, the plug is keyed backwards for this board. Do not force it and do not plug it in. Take it back to the repair shop and have the two wires swapped in the header — a two-minute job. This is exactly why you have four spare leads.
  5. Only when red → + and black → − are confirmed, plug it in.

7d · Charging and expected runtime

The Heltec V3 has a lithium charger built in. With the battery connected, plug in USB and it charges; a charge LED on the board indicates progress. There is nothing to configure.

RoleTypical drawOn one 2000 mAh cell

Both numbers are far beyond any single field test, which is the point — battery life is not something you will need to think about in Phase 1. Charge both cells the night before and forget them.

Cold weather note for later

These runtimes are for a warm room. Lithium cells lose a large fraction of their usable capacity below 0 °C, which is why the Phase 2 cold-chamber test exists and why the flight battery is a different chemistry. For Phase 1, ambient Pune conditions, this is a non-issue.

Checkpoint 07

The TX board runs from the LiPo alone with no USB attached, screen lit, still counting and still being received. You have confirmed red → + before it was ever powered. Both cells charge from USB.

STEP 08

Taking it outside

Everything up to here was rehearsal. Restore TX_DBM to 22, re-upload the TX sketch, charge both cells, and go and find out what these radios actually do.

What the physics says you should get

This is the number that reframes the whole exercise. With clear line of sight, 22 dBm, and these small antennas:

The lesson hiding in that table

Even at 20 km you have 39 dB of margin in free space. Distance is not your enemy. If a test fails, it will not be because 20 km is too far — it will be because something was in the way. Every decision in the field is therefore about height and sight-lines, never about turning the power up.

How high you have to be

Radio does not travel in a pencil-thin line. It needs an elongated ellipse of clear space around the straight line — the Fresnel zone. Clip the middle of it with a rooftop or a ridge and you can lose 6–20 dB even though you can see the other end perfectly. On top of that, the earth itself bulges up between you.

LinkFresnel radius60% of it (the working rule)Earth bulgeTotal clearance the path needs at midpoint
10 km29.4 m17.6 m1.5 m19.1 m
20 km41.6 m24.9 m5.9 m30.8 m

In practical terms: for the 10 km city test, both ends want to be on roofs at least six or seven floors up, and nothing taller may sit near the midpoint. For the 20 km test, use terrain — put one end on high ground so the geometry is given to you for free rather than bought with building height.

The procedure, same every time

  1. Plan on a map first. Draw the straight line between the two points and look at what it crosses. If a taller building or a ridge sits near the middle, pick different points before you leave the house.
  2. Set up RX first, at the fixed end, with your laptop. Confirm it says listening.... Antenna vertical, not lying on a parapet, ideally taped to something that holds it upright and clear of the wall.
  3. Take TX to the far point. Battery only. Antenna vertical. Hold it up and out, or tape it to a pole — do not hold it flat against your chest, you are a bag of saltwater and you will absorb several dB.
  4. Wait two minutes. At 20 s intervals that is six packets. Do not conclude anything from one packet.
  5. Record the log line below before moving. The serial monitor on the RX laptop is already printing RSSI, SNR and the ok/miss counters — select and copy it.
  6. Sweep the antenna if the link is marginal: tilt it a few degrees, move two metres sideways, raise it half a metre. Watch RSSI change. This teaches you more about radio in five minutes than any amount of reading.

Compare your typical RSSI against the predicted value in the table above. The gap between them is your real-world excess loss — buildings, trees, humidity, cable losses, antenna imperfection. That single number, measured at 3, 5, 10 and 20 km, is the entire deliverable of Phase 1. It is what turns the flight link budget from an estimate into a measurement.

Practical notes for the day
  • Two phones on a call between the two ends beats trying to coordinate by message. You want to be able to say “tilt it now” and hear the result.
  • Rooftop access takes longer than the test. Ask permission the day before. Carry the printed page rather than explaining a balloon project from memory.
  • Do the easy one first. 3 km, then 5, then 10. If 3 km fails you have a setup problem, not a range problem, and you want to discover that close to home.
  • Bring the USB cable and a power bank even though the LiPo will last all day — being able to re-flash a board in the field once saved a trip is worth the weight.
Optional, and worth the extra hour: the Yagi A/B

If the TX900-BM-9 Yagi (12 dBi, ground station only) has arrived, run the 20 km test twice from the same two points — once with the 2.2 dBi omni on the receiver, once with the Yagi aimed. You should measure roughly +10 dB of improvement in RSSI.

That single comparison validates three things at once: the antenna is what it claims to be, your measurement method is sound, and the link-budget model that the whole flight plan rests on actually predicts reality. It is the cheapest calibration you will ever run.

The Yagi needs an N-male to SMA-male adapter to meet your pigtail — it will not connect without one. Keep the coax run under a metre and put the Heltec at the antenna, running USB down to the laptop.

Checkpoint 08 — Phase 1 complete

You have four log sheets: 3 km, 5 km, 10 km in the city, and 20 km open-country or mountain. Each records measured RSSI and SNR against predicted. You know your real excess loss and you know what breaks the link. Phase 2 — the cold chamber — can begin.

The rules, on one card

Print this or photograph it. Everything here is something that costs money, time, or skin when ignored.

Never
  • Power a LoRa board with no antenna attached.
  • Cut, strip or shorten the battery's own wires.
  • Plug a battery in without confirming red → + against the board printing.
  • Pull a u.FL connector off by its cable.
  • Tighten an SMA with pliers.
  • Charge a swollen, punctured or dropped LiPo, or leave any LiPo charging unattended.
  • Use 868.1 MHz — that is the European channel, outside India's 865–867 band.
Always
  • Antenna on, then power. In that order, every time.
  • Both antennas vertical.
  • Identical radio settings on both boards.
  • Label the boards TX and RX.
  • Change one thing at a time when something breaks.
  • Write down RSSI, SNR and position before you move.
  • Restore TX_DBM 22 before going outdoors.

Where this sits in the campaign

Phase 1 has one job, and it is not “test the radio”. It is to make sure that your first working radio link contains no unknowns — so that when the Raspberry Pi, the sensors, the cold and the altitude arrive in Phases 2 and 3, every failure you see is caused by the thing you just added, and not by something you never actually verified. Six parts, ₹5,995, no soldering iron of your own, no Pi required.

When your four log sheets are filled in, come back to the main dossier and start Phase 2.
