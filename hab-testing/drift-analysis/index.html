The headline number for the application

We request a release area of 60 NM (110 km) radius centred on Jejuri. That contains 4,949 of 4,950 simulated flights — every measured October and November atmosphere over Pune in the last decade, flown against a vehicle whose parameters were randomised across their full manufacturing and operating tolerance.

The flight is airborne for a median of 121 minutes and reaches its highest point at T+70 minutes, having drifted only 21 km by then. Most of the drift happens during the parachute descent, not the ascent.

Two matters that need a decision, raised now rather than later
  1. FL750 does not cover this flight. The balloon bursts at approximately 31.3 km — that is FL1027. Our own mission altitude of 25 km is FL820. A FL750 block tops out at 22.86 km, below both. We believe our original request was specified incorrectly and should be revised to a block up to FL1100. See §9.
  2. The 100–150 NM figure is real, but it belongs to a failure case, not the nominal flight. It is what happens if the balloon does not burst. We propose to remove that case with a redundant cut-down. See §2 and §8.
02

Reconciling the 100–150 NM estimate

Wing Commander Piyush's rough figure of 100–150 NM is roughly five times our nominal median. That gap is not a disagreement about arithmetic — it is a difference in which flight is being described, and the difference is worth setting out plainly because it identifies the one failure mode that genuinely matters.

ScenarioMedian95th pctWorstComment
Nominal burst flight, Oct–Nov 19 NM35 NM62 NM What we intend to fly.
Nominal, but launched in January 49 NM71 NM83 NM Subtropical jet at full strength. We are not proposing to launch then.
Balloon fails to burst, floats 3 h 61 NM186 NM270 NM This is the 100–150 NM band.
Fails to burst, floats 6 h 116 NM354 NM507 NM Unbounded in practice. Must be designed out.
Our reading of this

The Wing Commander's instinct is correct and we would rather build to it than argue with it. A free balloon that does not terminate is genuinely a 100–150 NM problem, and an air traffic authority is right to size the hazard on that basis unless the operator can show the case has been removed.

So we are not asking to be assessed on the nominal number alone. We are proposing to eliminate the float case with an independent, redundant cut-down device (§8), and to accept a licence condition that ties our clearance to it. With that in place the flight is bounded by physics rather than by hope, and the 60 NM figure becomes the honest one.

03

Method and data

The request was for industry-standard tools. The method below is the one used by the Cambridge University Spaceflight predictor, by SondeHub (the tracking network that serves the international radiosonde and amateur balloon community), and by NOAA's HYSPLIT model in its single-trajectory mode. It is deliberately simple, because for a free balloon the physics is simple and the uncertainty lives entirely in the weather.

The vehicle is a passive tracer

A free balloon has no propulsion and no aerodynamic control. Its horizontal velocity is therefore identically the wind velocity at whatever altitude it currently occupies. The entire trajectory problem reduces to two integrals:

ascent    dx/dt = u(z)     dy/dt = v(z)     dz/dt = +w_ascent(z)
descent   dx/dt = u(z)     dy/dt = v(z)     dz/dt = -w_descent(z)

where w_ascent follows from constant free lift on an unstressed latex envelope (the gas is at ambient pressure, so displaced air mass is constant and the rate scales as ρ-1/6), and w_descent is parachute terminal velocity √(2mg / ρ Cd A). Both are integrated in 50 m altitude steps, finer than the radiosonde's own vertical resolution.

The wind field is measured, not modelled

This is the part we would ask the Wing Commander to weigh most heavily. We have not used a forecast, a reanalysis, or a climatological average as the input to the trajectory. Each simulated flight replays one real radiosonde ascent released from Pune — an instrument that physically measured the wind at every level of the column on a specific day.

A useful robustness result

Adding the full vehicle dispersion moved the 95th percentile from 63.4 km to 64.3 km — under one kilometre. The answer is set by the weather, not by our engineering tolerances. That is worth stating because it means the estimate does not depend on trusting our workmanship: any vehicle of roughly this mass under roughly this parachute produces the same footprint.

04

Why the drift over Pune is small

There is a specific and checkable physical reason the numbers come out low, and it is the single most useful thing in this document. The wind column over Pune in October and November reverses direction partway up, and the balloon spends time in both halves.

LayerMean direction of travelMean speed95th pct speed
0–5 km, boundary layer and lower tropospheretoward W–NW3–6 m/s≤ 11 m/s
6–16 km, subtropical jet remnanttoward NE6–14 m/s29 m/s
17–20 km, tropopause transitionreversing7–8 m/s16 m/s
21–31 km, tropical stratospheric easterlytoward W8–11 m/s27 m/s

Two consequences follow, and both matter for airspace planning:

  • The maximum wind anywhere in the column is modest. The strongest mean layer is 14 m/s (27 kt) at 12–13 km, and the 95th percentile is 29 m/s (56 kt). This is not a jet-stream environment. A mid-latitude winter launch site would routinely see 50–80 m/s at the same altitude, which is where the 100–150 NM rule of thumb comes from.
  • The reversal is partly self-cancelling. This is why the landing bearing distribution in §7 is bimodal — roughly half of flights land north-east and roughly a quarter land west, depending on which layer dominated on the day.

Time spent in controlled airspace

The vehicle is below FL450 for 38 minutes on the way up and 26 minutes on the way down — 64 minutes of a 121-minute flight. During the ascent leg it is within 12 km of the pad at the median and 29 km at the 95th percentile, so the airspace actually affected during the climb is a small cylinder, not the full landing footprint. The descent leg is the wider one, and it is also the slower and more predictable one, because by then the vehicle is under a parachute with a known terminal velocity and is transmitting live GPS.

Reading this table

October and November are not a convenience — they are the annual minimum of the drift distribution, at roughly a third of the January median. The post-monsoon column over Pune is the quietest genuinely flyable atmosphere of the year: the monsoon easterly has collapsed and the winter subtropical jet has not yet built.

It is also worth noting what the table does not show. In no month, in ten years of measured atmospheres, does a nominal burst flight from Jejuri exceed 90 NM. A 100–150 NM outcome does not arise from weather alone; it requires the vehicle to fail to terminate.

08

Failure cases and how we close them

The nominal number is only meaningful if the failure cases are bounded. These are the three that change the answer, with the mitigation we propose to be held to.

F1 — Balloon fails to burst

A latex envelope that stops expanding — because it was under-filled, because a cold tropopause limited its growth, or because a manufacturing variance gave it an unusually high burst diameter — can float instead of bursting. It then travels at stratospheric wind speed for as long as it stays buoyant.

Float durationMedian95th pctWorstReaches the Arabian Sea?
0 h (nominal burst)19 NM35 NM62 NMNo
3 h61 NM186 NM270 NMYes, frequently
6 h116 NM354 NM507 NMYes
Mitigation — we propose this as a licence condition

A redundant cut-down: two independent devices, each able to sever the balloon from the payload train, on separate power and separate control paths.

  • Primary: command cut-down over the telemetry link, triggered by the ground station on ATC instruction or on our own geofence breach.
  • Backup: an autonomous timer plus altitude trigger on its own battery and its own microcontroller, which fires at a pre-declared elapsed time regardless of the state of every other system, including total loss of the primary computer.

With a backup timer set to T+90 minutes — 20 minutes after nominal burst — the float case collapses back into the nominal envelope, because the vehicle physically cannot remain aloft long enough to leave it. We would accept a condition requiring us to demonstrate both devices firing before the flight is cleared.

F2 — Parachute failure

The payload descends ballistically. Counter-intuitively this reduces drift, because the descent takes minutes instead of 50 minutes, so the landing point moves closer to the burst point — approximately 21 km from the pad at the median. It is a ground-safety concern, addressed by a 2.2 kg payload in a crushable expanded-polystyrene enclosure with no hard corners and a terminal energy at nominal descent of about 23 J. It is not an airspace concern.

F3 — Loss of tracking

Does not affect where the vehicle goes, only our ability to report it. Mitigated by three independent tracking chains on separate batteries, separate microcontrollers and separate radios: the primary telemetry link, an independent LoRa beacon, and a cellular tracker that reports position by SMS once the payload descends into GSM coverage below roughly 3 km.

We need to revise our own request

FL750 does not cover this flight. The vehicle passes through FL750 at T+59 minutes and keeps climbing for another 11 minutes. Our original application understated the required block, and we would like to correct it to a request for a block up to FL1100 (110,000 ft / 33.5 km), which covers nominal burst plus the full +2 km manufacturing dispersion with margin.

If only FL450 can be granted

We have simulated that case as well, because a cut-down at FL450 is a flight we could usefully fly. It becomes a different mission — it cannot carry the high-altitude objective — but it is a complete engineering rehearsal and it is worth flying on its own terms.

We would be glad to fly that as a first flight under a Pune-only clearance, and use it to demonstrate the cut-down, the tracking chains and the recovery procedure before asking anyone to approve the full-altitude profile.

Cross-checks against flown hardware

A simulation that has not been checked against reality is an opinion. Three independent comparisons, in increasing order of relevance to this flight.

Against published amateur and educational flight statistics

Our descent is longer than the quoted 20–30 minutes because we fly a deliberately oversized parachute for a light payload — a design choice that trades drift for landing energy, and one we would defend on ground-safety grounds.

Against the Indian national precedent

The TIFR Balloon Facility, Hyderabad — the National Balloon Facility, jointly managed by TIFR and ISRO — has flown more than 500 stratospheric balloons from Indian soil since the 1960s. Its published operational recovery ranges are 486 km west, 396 km north, 364 km east and 392 km south of the base, with the facility noting that flights are conducted regionally because of the air corridor allocated by the civil aviation authorities.

Why this comparison is favourable to us

Those ranges are for zero-pressure balloons that float for many hours by design — a fundamentally longer-ranging vehicle than a latex sounding balloon that bursts at T+70 minutes. Our 95th-percentile footprint of 64 km is roughly a seventh of the western corridor that TIFR and ISRO already operate within, on a plateau with comparable terrain and at a similar latitude. TIFR also operates its winter campaign in October–December, which independently corroborates our choice of window.

Against the standard operational predictor

The method in §3 is the same passive-tracer layer integration used by the Cambridge University Spaceflight (CUSF) predictor, exposed publicly as the SondeHub predictor at predict.sondehub.org, which is the tool the international balloon community uses operationally and which is validated daily against several hundred real radiosonde flights worldwide. The difference is the wind input: the SondeHub tool runs on the current NOAA GFS forecast, which is correct for a specific launch day but tells you nothing about the envelope. Our run substitutes ten years of measured Pune soundings to produce the climatological envelope an airspace application needs.

We propose to file both. This document is the envelope. At T−24 hours we will run the same trajectory against the live GFS forecast through the SondeHub predictor and submit the specific predicted track and landing point for the actual launch day, so ATC is never asked to rely on climatology alone.

11

What we commit to

Offered as proposed licence conditions. We would rather be bound by these in writing than be granted a discretionary clearance.

  1. Redundant cut-down. Two independent termination devices on separate power and separate control paths, backup timer set to T+90 min, both demonstrated firing before flight.
  2. T−24 h forecast trajectory. A specific predicted track and landing point filed with ATC the day before launch, using live GFS winds.
  3. No-launch on westward drift. If the T−24 h prediction places the landing point with any significant westward component toward the Sahyadri scarp or beyond, we stand down. The Arabian Sea is 133 km west; we will not fly toward it.
  4. No-launch outside the window. October and November only for this flight.
  5. Live position reporting. A telephone line held open to Pune ATC for the duration, with position, altitude and rate reported at an interval of ATC's choosing, and immediate notification of burst and of touchdown.
  6. Cut-down on ATC instruction. Unconditionally, at any point in the flight, without discussion.
  7. Payload conspicuity and safety. Under 2.5 kg, expanded polystyrene, no hard corners, high-visibility, labelled in Marathi, Hindi and English with a 24-hour telephone number and an explicit statement that the object is a harmless scientific instrument.
  8. Local police pre-notification in every taluka inside the 95th-percentile ring, before launch, so a found payload is a logged expected object rather than a bomb scare.
  9. Post-flight report to ATC within 72 hours: actual track, actual landing point, and measured versus predicted drift, so the next application rests on our own flown data rather than on this document.
