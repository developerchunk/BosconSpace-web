From the HAB-1 cameras, 31 October 2026. Edited.

Our first stratospheric balloon carried the Boscon flight computer, telemetry radio and an onboard language model to 19,625 m above Solapur, and came home 62.7 km away.

Released at 12:37 IST. Burst at 19.6 km seventy minutes later. Under its parachute for fifty-four minutes. Down 62.7 km north-east of the pad, where our live predictor, re-run from every packet in flight, had said it would come down.

    Simulation

    What we predicted, and what flew.

    The morning of launch, the Boscon HAB predictor flew the balloon 300 times through the GFS forecast and the ECMWF ensemble. Every one of those flights burst between 26.8 and 32.5 km (5th to 95th percentile). HAB-1 burst at 19.6 km and fell more slowly than modelled. After the flight we re-ran the same predictor on the day's winds to see which mattered.

    We also asked the predictor what HAB-1 would have done had the balloon held to 25 or 30 km. Every run uses the measured climb of 4.54 m/s and one descent rate fitted to HAB-1's own fall: 3.44 m/s at sea level, faster in thinner air. Only the burst height changes. With these settings and the real 19.6 km burst, it lands 3.3 km from the real touchdown.

    Between 19.6 and 30 km the day's winds carried the balloon about 8 km west-north-west, so a higher burst lands at about the same range, but further round to the north. Above 19.6 km the climb rate and the descent law are extrapolated, not measured. One run each, no Monte Carlo. Both tracks are on the map under Layers.

    Flight computer

    A compute device running an LLM, sealed inside the payload, at 19.6 km.

    The Pi logged every sensor, recorded two cameras and ran the language model for the whole flight. Thin air carries little heat away, so it ran hot from the middle of the climb, and kept working.

    Processor temperature
    45.2 to 86.45 °C

        Findings

        What flying taught us.

        1. 01

          The balloon burst at 19.6 km, not 30.

          It climbed steadily at 4.3 to 4.9 m/s to the end, so it was not leaking: it failed suddenly. The cause is not known yet.

        2. 02

          It landed 20 km from the morning's prediction, and a few hundred metres from the live one.

          The live prediction, recomputed from every packet, moved about 15 km north-east in the 92 s after the burst, to within 1.3 km of the real landing, and then stayed within about a kilometre of it.

        3. 03

          The parachute came down slower than modelled.

          About 2.7 m/s at touchdown against 7.0 m/s predicted. That alone moved the landing about 10 km.

        4. 04

          Thin air is a poor heatsink.

          The Pi spent about 65 minutes at or above 80 °C and throttled, but lost no data. The next box needs a thermal path to the outside.

        5. 05

          The radio link held to 64.8 km from the pad.

          The last packet reached the ground station from 2,109 m up, on the way down. The Pi logged the beacon on a wire the whole way, so the record has no gap.

        6. 06

          A small model can report from the edge of space.

          Copying facts from sensors worked. Reasoning about them did not always. A checker that compares each answer with the measurement is worth keeping.

        Not done yet

        Most of the video is not reviewed. The cause of the burst is not known.
