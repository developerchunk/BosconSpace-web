AI at the edge of space. Written in Kotlin.

In the last week of October 2026, Boscon Space flies a language model to the stratosphere on HAB-1. Its flight computer, from the pins to the model, is one Kotlin program. This page is for JetBrains.

Boscon Space HAB-1 · For JetBrains Scroll

Compute belongs where the data is made. Before it belongs in orbit, it belongs under a balloon. HAB-1 carries a small computer, a radio and a language model to twenty-five kilometres, answers questions from the ground, and comes home. It is the first node of the Boscon mesh, and every line of its software is Kotlin.

HAB-1

A question goes up by radio. The answer comes back from the stratosphere.

01 / 03
The flight

A balloon lifts a small box to the edge of space.

A hydrogen weather balloon carries an insulated payload to between 25 and 30 kilometres, where the air is a hundred times thinner and the daytime sky is black. It bursts, the payload descends under a parachute, and the team recovers it.

The software

One Kotlin program owns the whole payload.

Compiled with Kotlin/Native into a single small file. It drives the computer's pins and the radio chip directly, reads the sensors, runs the language model, keeps a flight log that survives power loss, and answers questions. No virtual machine, no second language in the loop.

The ground

The same Kotlin runs the ground station.

A Compose for Desktop console watches the payload live: pins, radio, charts, prompts and answers. It shares the payload's own code for the radio messages and the data analysis, so nothing is written twice.

What we built

Kotlin, from the pins to the model.

01  Hardware access

The pins, the buses, the radio

A Kotlin layer that talks to the Linux kernel directly: switching pins, counting pulses, the two standard chip-to-chip buses, and a driver for the Semtech SX1262 radio that matches the established C++ library byte for byte, so our frames are received by unchanged equipment.

02  Intelligence

The model, the agent, the analysis

A 1.5-billion-parameter model runs inside the Kotlin program with the llama.cpp kernel linked in. An agent lets it read live sensors with a single line, and refuses to run when the computer is too hot or the battery too low. Analysis code finds the radiation peak, the burst and the free fall, and flags any sensor that lies.

03  Operations

The flight process and the console

One process ticks once a second: sensors, analysis, beacon, log, watchdog, with the model answering on its own thread. The Mac console shows all of it live and can build, deploy and reflash the payload over the air of the bench. Every panel reads the real board.

Measured on the bench, 11 October 2026

Not a plan. Running.

  • Model speed11.9 tok/s

    Generated from Kotlin on the Raspberry Pi 5. The C++ benchmark on the same board gives 12.06. The binding costs nothing.

  • Beacon timing1,000 ms ± 6

    Between position frames, measured from the flight log, while the model was busy answering a question.

  • A real exchange938.5 hPa

    "What is the air pressure around you?" The model called its sensor tool and quoted the live reading exactly.

  • Hardware4,000 / 4,000

    Pulse edges counted without loss; 1,280 bytes round-tripped over the chip bus at 8 MHz; radio frames received by the unchanged receiver.

  • Resilience40 s

    From a deliberately frozen flight program to a watchdog restart. Unplugging the radio mid-flight: it kept answering and re-attached by itself.

  • Code2,814 lines

    Of Kotlin across nine modules, with 166 lines of C++ on a helper board and 333 lines of scripts. All of it public.

Progress

Cleared to fly.

  1. Now

    HAB-1, first launch: 24 October 2026

    Clearances from the Government of India, the Ministry of Civil Aviation and the Indian Air Force are in hand. The launch window is 24 October, with 31 October held as the reserve date. Thirty days of continuous ground testing precede it.

  2. Next

    November and December: the mesh in the stratosphere

    Further flights carrying the language model together with inter-communication modules, talking to multiple ground stations at once: the Boscon mesh exercised under balloons before it goes to orbit.

  3. Then

    First relay in orbit

    One relay in low-Earth orbit, carrying third-party traffic to the ground through the mesh.

  4. Then

    AI nodes

    Compute joins the mesh. The first models run on data in orbit, on the software proven on HAB-1.

A story the Kotlin community has not seen yet.

Balloon and small-satellite computers run Python or C. We chose Kotlin for all of it, met every edge of the Linux ARM64 target on the way, and wrote each one down: C interop against raw kernel interfaces, linking a C++ inference library, the handful of missing POSIX bindings, a Tier 2 target pinned for a flight campaign. The result is a working flight computer and a ground console in one language, in the open, about to fly.

  1. 01

    A Kotlin/Native point of contact.

    We have worked around every edge we hit and documented it. A conversation with the people who build the toolchain makes the next ones faster, and gives you a real deployment at the edge of the platform.

  2. 02

    Tell the story together.

    When it flies, we publish the code, the flight data and what we learned, mistakes included. A blog post, a talk, a case study, in whatever form fits JetBrains.
