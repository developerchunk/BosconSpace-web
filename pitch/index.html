Boscon Space · Investor briefing · October 2026

Data centres in orbit.
Connected by a mesh.

The next place to build AI infrastructure is above the clouds, where the sun never sets. Boscon Space builds the computers that go there, and the network that joins them to each other and to the ground.

One line: we are building orbital data centres and the mesh network that connects them. The rest of the deck explains why that is where computing is heading, who else is going there, what it costs, and where we are today.
01  The problem on Earth

AI is running out of power, not chips.

415 TWh

Electricity used by the world's data centres in 2024. About 1.5% of all electricity.

IEA estimate
~945 TWh

Where the same number is heading by 2030. More than double in six years.

IEA projection
4.4% → 12%

Share of all US electricity going to data centres in 2023, and the top of the 2028 range.

Berkeley Lab, measured and projected

New data centres now wait years for a grid connection, land and water. Power has become the bottleneck for AI.

Sources: IEA, Energy and AI (Apr 2025); Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, US Data Center Energy Usage Report (Dec 2024).

Familiar to anyone in IT: every hyperscaler is now constrained by power and grid connections. That pressure is the reason serious companies are looking up.
02  Why space

In the right orbit, the sun never sets.

Power

A solar panel in a dawn-to-dusk orbit sees the sun almost all the time, with no night, clouds or weather. Google estimates it produces up to 8x more energy than the same panel on the ground.

Cooling

Heat is radiated straight into the cold of deep space. No chillers, no cooling towers, no water.

Space

No land to buy, no permits to wait for, no queue for the grid.

Source: Google Research, Project Suncatcher (Nov 2025), company estimate.

Think of it as moving the server room to where the power plant is. The physics argument is settled; the cost argument is the next slides.
03  The problem in space

Satellites are online for minutes, offline for hours.

A satellite can only send data while it passes over one of its ground stations. For the rest of the orbit, its data waits on board. And satellites now collect far more than they can send down.

~100x

How much more data each new generation of satellite sensors produces.

ESA, expert estimate
3x to 5x

How much faster each generation of download links gets.

ESA, expert estimate

The fix is the one IT already uses at the edge: process the data where it is made, and send down the answer, not the raw file.

Source: ESA, quoted in HPCwire (Oct 2020).

04  It already works

Computing in orbit is not science fiction.

In 2021, a server on the International Space Station analysed a human genome on board. Instead of sending the raw data home, it sent only the result.

182 GB → 13 MB

Raw data versus what was actually sent down. About 14,000x less.

Measured, reported by Microsoft and HPE

Since then, an Nvidia H100 data-centre GPU has run an AI model in orbit (Starcloud, Nov 2025), and Google has launched its first AI chips on a satellite (Oct 2026, reported).

Sources: Microsoft Azure blog (2021), HPE Spaceborne Computer-2; CNBC (Dec 2025); NPR (Oct 2026).

Spaceborne Computer-2 on the ISS. NASA

08  Cost, now and next

Launch is getting cheap the way storage did.

The price of putting one kilogram into orbit decides everything. It has already fallen about 20x, and the next step is in flight.

  1. Space Shuttle~$54,500per kg, historical
  2. Falcon 9~$2,700per kg, today
  3. Starship eraunder $200per kg, mid-2030s target

Below about $200 per kg, power in orbit costs about the same as power on the ground. Starship, the rocket built for that price, reached orbit in September 2026.

Sources: NASA cost papers (Shuttle, Falcon 9 list price); Google Research, Suncatcher (Nov 2025), company projection. Photo: Starship launch seen from the ISS, NASA.
